Twelve questionsThe questions investors actually ask, including the awkward ones
01What is the genuine worst case here?

You fund a position, the company never lists, and it goes to zero. There is no floor, no insurance and no way to sell out early. That is the honest worst case, and it is the reason this should only ever be capital you can leave alone and, in the bad case, lose entirely.

02Why are there two company names?

You met Lumara Investment Partners LP through an advert. Lumara is the marketing arm that partners with Collins Investment Partners. Collins Investment Partners LP is the firm behind the offer: a registered investment adviser specialising in late-stage private technology, and the one taking your call. Every document, fee and disclosure you receive will carry the Collins name, and you should read the name on anything you are asked to sign.

03What am I actually buying?

A position in the same round the firm takes, at the same price. The liquidity event is a listing or an acquisition. The exact structure of the interest, any lock-up and the fee schedule are all set out in the step 3 documents, in writing, before anyone commits. If any of those three is unclear to you when you read them, that is a reason to stop and ask, not to sign.

04Why has my advisor never shown me this?

Because the plumbing does not reach them. Late-stage rounds are allocated privately, in seven-figure blocks, to firms already positioned to take them. Unless a firm splits its own position, there is nothing at $25,000 for an advisor to put in front of you.

05What returns should I expect?

No number is going in writing here, and you should be careful with anyone who offers you one. Target returns are hypothetical, they may not be achieved, and you can lose your principal.

What can be told to you is the screen: $5 billion or more in valuation, $50 million or more in revenue, 20%+ growth. On the call you get the research behind each name. Separately, Lumara's own published record is that 9 out of 10 of the companies it has invested in have gone public. That is a record, not a forecast, and it is not a claim about anything offered to you.

06How long is the money committed?

Expect 1 to 3 years or longer, until the company lists or is acquired. These are illiquid positions. There is no exchange to sell them on, no redemption window and no early exit anyone can promise. It should never be money you need in that window.

07Is an IPO guaranteed?

No. No company is obligated to list, and a position can lose value or fail entirely. What is on offer is a screen, published research and judgment about which companies are positioned to list. Not a date, and not an outcome.

08Do I need to be accredited?

Yes. Net worth over $1 million excluding your home, or income of $200,000 a year, $300,000 jointly, for each of the past two years. It is verified with documents after the call: financial statements, tax returns or a CPA letter. After the call, never on it.

09Why is the minimum $25,000?

Because the firm takes the whole institutional position first, and a block is a fixed size. $25,000 is the smallest piece that still works inside rounds otherwise sold in seven-figure lots. Below that, neither the arithmetic nor the paperwork makes sense for either side.

10How does the firm make money?

A management fee, plus a performance fee charged only on gains above a set threshold. The full schedule is in writing before you sign anything. Better you ask now than wonder later, and if a number is not clear on the call, ask for it out loud.

11What happens at tax time, and what do I hear from you?

These structures generally issue a K-1. Investors get quarterly reports plus notice of anything material. Between those, there is genuinely nothing to watch: private positions are repriced only when the company raises, so there is no daily number and nothing you need to do.

And because nobody else will put this on a page: here is every outcome a private position can have.

Total loss
The company fails and the position is worth nothing. A real outcome in private markets, not a theoretical one.
Marked down
A later round is priced below the one you entered. A private position can be marked down as easily as up.
Stays private longer
No failure, no exit, just years more than anyone expected. Your capital stays where it is throughout.
Acquired
Bought by another company. Terms are set by that deal, not by you, and can be cash, stock or both.
Lists publicly
The outcome the whole thesis is built on, and the one nobody can promise. Even then, a lock-up usually applies.

Search three

"Is pre-IPO investing a scam?"

There are real bad actors in this corner of the market. The pattern is almost always one of three things: a synthetic wrapper sold as if it were real equity, a fee stack nobody will put in writing, or an exit date presented as a certainty.

So test all three on us. Ask what the instrument legally is, ask for every fee in writing before step 3, and ask whether any date is being promised. If an answer is vague, that is your answer.

Search four

"Has anyone lost money doing this?"

Yes. Private companies fail, positions get marked down, and exits take longer than anyone wants. That sentence sits under the success stories on the Lumara site for exactly this reason, and it belongs here too.

Any firm in this market claiming a clean record is either new or not being straight with you. Ask how it has gone for the positions that did not work.
